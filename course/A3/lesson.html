History

The first mention of binary search for computers is by John Mauchly, one of the designers of ENIAC, in the Moore School lectures of 1946. A version that works for every length was published only in 1962, as Jon Bentley recalls in Programming Pearls, where he also reports that most professional programmers he asked could not write it correctly. In 2006 Joshua Bloch found that Java's own binary search failed on arrays of more than about a billion elements: (low + high) / 2 overflowed. Hence mid := left + (right-left)/2.

The problem it fixes

Finding a value in a sorted list of a billion entries by looking at each one is a billion steps.

How it works

Look at the middle: the answer is in the left half or in the right half, so throw the other half away. Thirty halvings take a billion down to one: O(log n). The trick to getting it right is the promise the loop keeps ("if the answer exists, it is in [left, right)"), and checking that every update keeps it. Your old bugs from our chat lessons, right-left > 1 and left = mid, both break that promise. The same halving finds the first n for which a property becomes true (exercise 2).

Where it is used

  • Go's sort.Search and slices.BinarySearch.
  • Database indexes and B-trees (D9) search inside each node.
  • Profilers and debuggers turning an address into a function name search a sorted table of start addresses (the floor of D7, done with a slice).
  • Numerical methods: the bisection method finds a root of a function.
