History

Bernard Galler and Michael Fischer described the structure in 1964 in "An improved equivalence algorithm": each group is a tree, and the root names the group. How fast it really is took years to understand. In 1975 Robert Tarjan proved that with both of its tricks, a long sequence of operations costs only the inverse Ackermann function per operation, a number below 5 for any input that could ever exist.

The problem it fixes

Things get connected two at a time, and the question keeps coming: "are these two connected yet, through any chain?" A search through the graph (A10) answers once, in O(n); asked a million times, it is far too slow.

How it works

Every element points to a parent; a root points to itself. Find follows the parents to the root. Union finds both roots and puts one under the other. Two tricks keep the trees flat: union by size (the smaller tree goes under the bigger) and path compression (after a Find, everything on the path points straight to the root).

Where it is used

  • Kruskal's minimum spanning tree (exercise 2, and A14): "would this link close a loop?"
  • Connected regions of an image: pixels of the same colour joined to their neighbours.
  • Type inference in compilers: "these two type variables must be the same type".
  • Networks: which machines can reach each other as links come up.
