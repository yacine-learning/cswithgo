  • On sorted data, compare with the middle and throw away the half that cannot hold the answer: O(log n). A million items in about 20 steps.
  • The rule that makes it correct: the answer, if it exists, is always inside [left, right]. So for left <= right, and move to mid + 1 or mid − 1 so the range always shrinks.
  • The most useful form finds the first position where a condition becomes true: sort.Search, git bisect, "the first log line after 14:00".

The problem

You look for a word in a dictionary, a timestamp in a sorted log, the commit that broke the build. Checking every item is O(n), and when each check takes minutes (a build, a test run) n checks is not an option.

Where it comes from

The first recorded mention of binary search for computers is by John Mauchly, one of the designers of ENIAC, in the Moore School lectures of 1946. It then took until 1962 before a version that works for every length was published, as Jon Bentley recalls in Programming Pearls, where he also reports that most professional programmers he asked could not write it correctly. In 2006 Joshua Bloch found that the binary search in Java's standard library, which he had written from Bentley's book, failed on arrays of more than about a billion elements: (low + high) / 2 overflowed. It had been there for years. Hence mid := left + (right-left)/2.

The idea

When you did this in February, you were surprised you could not have invented it yourself. Nobody is expected to: it took the field 16 years to publish a correct version. What you need is the invariant (the answer is in [left, right]) and the habit of testing the edges: the first element, the last, a missing one.
