History

Rudolf Bayer and Edward McCreight invented the B-tree at Boeing Scientific Research Laboratories; their report "Organization and maintenance of large ordered indices" is dated July 1970. They never said what the B stands for. McCreight recalled the lunch where they chose the name: Boeing (which they could not use without the lawyers), balanced, Bayer… Bayer likes to say that the more you think about what the B means, the better you understand B-trees.

The problem it fixes

A disk (or an SSD) reads whole blocks, thousands of bytes at a time, and each read is far slower than a memory access. A binary tree of a billion keys is 30 levels deep: 30 reads per lookup.

How it works

Make each node one disk block holding hundreds of keys, with a child between every two keys. With 500 children per node, a billion keys fit in 4 levels, and the top levels stay in memory. The tree grows at the root: when a node is full it splits in two and pushes its middle key up, so all leaves stay at the same depth. The common variant, the B+ tree, keeps all values in the leaves and links the leaves together for fast range scans.

Where it is used

  • The index of almost every relational database: PostgreSQL, MySQL (InnoDB), SQLite, Oracle.
  • File systems: Btrfs, NTFS, Apple's APFS use B-trees for their metadata.
  • Key-value stores such as BoltDB, written in Go.

No exercise: a correct B-tree with splits and merges is several hundred lines. Keep the idea: wide nodes the size of a disk block, a tree only 3 or 4 levels deep, all leaves at the same depth.

Sources
