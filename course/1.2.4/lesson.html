Keep this
  • A queue is first in, first out: add at the back, take from the front, both O(1) when done right.
  • A ring buffer does it in a fixed array: a head index that wraps around with %. No copying, no reallocation, bounded memory. Go's buffered channels keep their elements this way.
  • Queues decouple who produces work from who does it: job queues, message brokers, request backlogs, breadth-first search (1.4.2).

The problem

Requests arrive faster than you can serve them for a moment. They must wait, and fairly: first come, first served. You need a waiting line where adding and removing never gets slower as the line grows.

Where it comes from

The mathematics of waiting lines started with telephones. In 1908 Agner Krarup Erlang joined the Copenhagen Telephone Company and studied how calls arrive and wait for a free line; his first paper on it appeared in 1909, and queueing theory grew from it. The unit of telephone traffic is named the erlang after him. Every queue in a computer, from a network card's buffer to Kafka, is studied with his tools.

The idea

A slice used as a queue (q = q[1:] to dequeue) is simple and fine for short-lived queues. In a long-running one it has a cost you once discovered yourself: the front of the slice moves forward, but the array behind it stays allocated, and appends keep allocating new arrays. A ring buffer reuses the same slots forever.

Bounded queues are a feature

A ring buffer has a capacity. When it is full, the producer must wait or the item is refused. That is back-pressure: it tells the producer to slow down instead of letting memory grow until the process is OOM-killed. A Go channel with a buffer of 100 blocks the sender at 100 for the same reason.
