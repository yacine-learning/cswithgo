Moscow, 1959

Tony Hoare is 25, a British exchange student at Moscow State University, and his project is to get a computer to translate Russian into English. The Russian dictionary lives on magnetic tape, in alphabetical order. A tape is not a page you can flip open anywhere: it winds from one end to the other, and going back costs real time.

So before looking up the words of a sentence, he has to sort them. Then a single trip along the tape finds them all, in order. His first idea is the obvious one, moving values step by step past their neighbours, and it is far too slow. So he invents something else.

You are going to reinvent it with him. It takes one idea.

Put one number in its place

Before reading on

Here are seven numbers: 38 27 43 3 9 82 10. Forget about sorting all of them. Take only the last one, 10. Where does it end up once everything is sorted? And what is the least you have to do to the others to put it there?

Think about it, then open

10 ends up at index 2, because exactly two numbers are smaller than it (3 and 9). To put it there, you do not need the others sorted at all. You only need the smaller ones on its left and the bigger ones on its right, in any order. That is a partition, and 10 is the pivot.

Here is the partition the way you will write it in exercise 2 (the version from Nico Lomuto, later popularised by Jon Bentley). i marks the end of the "smaller than the pivot" zone; j walks over everything else:

Look at what is true at every line: everything before i is smaller than the pivot. It is the same kind of promise that fixed your binary search in 1.5.1 ("if the target is there, it is between left and right"). Keep the promise true at every step and the code is right; break it once and you get the off-by-one bugs you met there.

Now 10 is in its final place and will never move again. Left of it, 3 9: a smaller problem. Right of it, 38 27 82 43: another smaller problem. Do the same thing to each. That is all of quicksort.

The part Hoare could not write

"Do the same thing to each side" is recursion, and in 1959 Hoare had no way to say it. The language he had learned, Mercury Autocode, gave him no clean way to say it. He could do the sort, but he could not write it down clearly.

Back in England in 1960 he joined Elliott Brothers, a small computer maker. His boss asked him to write a library routine for Shellsort. Hoare said, rather timidly, that he knew a faster method. His boss bet him sixpence he didn't. Hoare won, but in his own words the method was "very difficult to explain".

The explanation came at Easter 1961, on a one-week course in Brighton about a new language, ALGOL 60, taught by Peter Naur, Edsger Dijkstra and Peter Landin. ALGOL 60 had recursion. Hoare saw that it was exactly what quicksort needed, and wrote the procedure that, he said, his whole career was founded on. It is the call stack from 1.3.1 doing the bookkeeping he used to do by hand.

In Go, the whole sort fits on your screen:

func QuickSort(a []int, lo, hi int) {
	if lo >= hi {
		return
	}
	p := partition(a, lo, hi)
	QuickSort(a, lo, p-1)
	QuickSort(a, p+1, hi)
}

And unlike merge sort (1.6.1), it needs no second array: everything happens by swapping inside the slice you already have. In 1960, when memory was counted in a few thousand words, that mattered a lot.

The trap

Predict, then run exercise 1

The pivot is always the last element. Exercise 1 sorts 4 000 numbers twice: once in random order, once already sorted. Which one do you expect to be faster? Write your guess down, then press Run.

After running: what happened

The sorted input is the disaster. Its last element is the largest, so the partition puts everything on the left and nothing on the right: each level peels off one element. That makes 4 000 levels of up to 4 000 comparisons, close to n²/2, against roughly n·log₂ n for random input. The run prints both reference numbers so you can compare.

The cruel part: "already sorted, or nearly" is one of the most common inputs there is.

Why not just take the middle element as pivot?

It fixes sorted input. But any fixed rule has some input that hits the bad case every time, and someone can build that input on purpose. A random pivot has no bad input, only bad luck, and bad luck repeated over thousands of levels is vanishingly rare. Run exercise 1 again and look at the third line.

Go goes one step further. Since Go 1.19, slices.Sort and sort.Sort use pdqsort ("pattern-defeating quicksort"): it notices when its pivots keep going badly and switches to heapsort (1.7.1), which is never O(n²).

Half the work, for a different question

Often you do not want everything sorted. You want one value: the median, the 90th percentile, the 10th smallest. Hoare saw it in the same 1961 paper: partition once, and look at where the pivot landed. If it is at position k, done. If not, the answer is on one side, and you can forget the other side entirely. He called it FIND; today it is quickselect, and it is exercise 3.

Exercises

What to keep in five years
  • Quicksort: put one pivot in its final place (smaller left, bigger right), then do the same to each side. In place, no extra array.
  • Fast on average (n log n), terrible with bad pivots (n², and sorted input is the classic trap). A random pivot makes the bad case a matter of luck.
  • Partition alone answers "what is the k-th smallest?" without sorting: quickselect.
