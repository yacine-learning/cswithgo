Keep this
  • Big O describes how the work grows when the input grows, not how many seconds a program takes.
  • Keep only what dominates when n is large: 3n² + 5n + 20 is O(n²). The ladder to know: O(1) < O(log n) < O(n) < O(n log n) < O(n²).
  • The doubling test: double n and watch the work (steps, or time on a big enough n). ×1: O(1) or O(log n). ×2: O(n). ×4: O(n²). A loop inside a loop over the same data is the usual O(n²); a map often brings it back to O(n).

The problem

Does a list of numbers contain the same number twice? Compare every pair: with 1 000 numbers it takes a blink; with 100 000 it takes minutes, and with 10 million, days. Nothing is broken: the method itself does not scale.

Timing it does not tell you why, or how bad it will get. The same program runs at different speeds on different machines, under different load. You need a way to talk about the method, independent of the machine.

Where it comes from

The notation is older than computers. Paul Bachmann, a German number theorist, wrote O(n) in 1894 to mean "a quantity of the order of n": the O is for Ordnung, order. Edmund Landau spread it in 1909 in his book on prime numbers.

Programmers met the same problem in the 1960s: machines differed in speed by factors of a hundred, so "it took 3 seconds" said more about the machine than about the algorithm. In 1976 Donald Knuth, author of The Art of Computer Programming, published a short note asking computer scientists to adopt O, Ω and Θ with precise meanings. They did; it is the vocabulary every algorithm book uses since.

The idea

Count the steps as a function of n, the size of the input, and keep only the part that wins when n is large. Constants do not matter: 10n and n both grow the same way when n doubles.

The doubling test comes from Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne's Algorithms: you do not need the maths to know the class of a program, you run it at n and 2n and read the ratio. That is what the exercises below do.

Why a map lookup is O(1) and a nested loop O(n²)

A map turns the key into a position with a hash function, then goes there directly: the work does not depend on how many keys the map holds (lesson 1.2.2 shows how). Comparing every pair of n values is n(n−1)/2 comparisons: for 1 000 values, about 500 000; for 100 000 values, about 5 000 000 000. That is the jump from a blink to minutes.

Big O is about large n. For 20 items, the "slower" method can win, because the constants Big O ignores (making a map costs something) dominate.
