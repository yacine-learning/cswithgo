Keep this
  • A heap is a tree where every parent is ≤ its children, so the minimum is always at the top. It lives in a slice: the children of i are at 2i+1 and 2i+2.
  • Push and Pop are O(log n) (a value climbs up or sinks down one path), peek is O(1). It is the standard priority queue.
  • Use it whenever you repeatedly need "the next smallest": schedulers and timers, top-k, merging k sorted streams (1.7.2), shortest paths with weights (Dijkstra). In Go: container/heap.

The problem

A scheduler where the most urgent process runs next, whatever the order they arrived in; an operating system's timers, where the next one to expire must be found instantly; a simulation that always handles the earliest pending event; the 10 most used names in a program. A sorted slice gives the minimum in O(1) but costs O(n) per insertion; an unsorted one inserts in O(1) but must scan for the minimum. You want both cheap.

Where it comes from

J. W. J. Williams, at Elliott Brothers in London (the company where Hoare had just written quicksort, 1.6.2), invented the binary heap in 1963 to build a sorting method, heapsort, published in 1964 as "Algorithm 232: Heapsort". The same year Robert Floyd showed how to build a heap from n values in O(n) instead of O(n log n). The heap outlived heapsort: today it is mostly used as a priority queue, and heapsort survives as the safety net inside Go's pdqsort (1.6.2).

The idea

A heap is only partly sorted: each parent is smaller than its children, nothing more. That is enough for the minimum to sit at the root, and cheap to maintain: after a push, the new value climbs until its parent is smaller; after a pop, the last value moves to the root and sinks until its children are larger. Both follow one path from the root to a leaf, and a tree packed in a slice is always balanced: log₂ n levels.
