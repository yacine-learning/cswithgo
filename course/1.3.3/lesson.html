Keep this
  • The three depth-first orders differ only in when the node is visited: pre-order before its children, in-order between left and right, post-order after its children.
  • In-order on a BST gives the keys sorted. Pre-order: parents first (copy a tree, mkdir -p, serialize). Post-order: children first (rm -rf, du, evaluating an expression).
  • Level by level is a different traversal: it needs a queue, it is breadth-first search (1.4.2).

The problem

A tree is not a line: "visit every node" leaves a choice of order, and the order is the whole point. You cannot delete a directory before its files, and you cannot create a file before its directory. To compute 3 × (4 + 1) you need 4 + 1 first.

Where it comes from

The orders were in use before computers, in how formulas are written. In the 1920s the Polish logician Jan Łukasiewicz wrote formulas with the operator first, × 3 + 4 1, which needs no brackets: that is a pre-order of the formula's tree, still called Polish notation. Its mirror image, 3 4 1 + ×, is reverse Polish notation, a post-order. In 1957 the Australian Charles Hamblin showed that a machine can evaluate it with a stack (1.2.3), which made compilers much easier to write. The way we write maths, 3 × (4 + 1), is the in-order, and it needs brackets.

The idea

All three are the same recursion (1.3.1): handle the left subtree, handle the right subtree, and visit the node itself before, between or after. When you worked on this before, the tricky part was how the call "comes back up" to do the right side: the left call returns, the frame resumes on the next line, and that line is the right call.
