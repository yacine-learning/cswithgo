History

In 1926 the Czech mathematician Otakar Borůvka worked out how to connect the towns of Moravia to the electricity network with the least wire; his solution, stated for an abstract weighted graph, is the first minimum spanning tree algorithm. Vojtěch Jarník published another method in 1930, rediscovered by Robert Prim in 1957 and by Dijkstra in 1959. Joseph Kruskal published his in 1956.

The problem it fixes

Connect every point of a network (towns, machines, pins on a circuit board) using the cheapest set of links. Any loop is wasted money: remove its most expensive link and everything is still connected.

How it works

The answer is a tree with n-1 links. Kruskal: take the links from the cheapest up, and keep each one unless it would close a loop; union-find (D13) answers "would it close a loop?" in almost constant time. Prim (Jarník): grow one tree from any node, always adding the cheapest link that leaves the tree, with a priority queue (D10). Both are greedy: they never revisit a choice, and here that is provably optimal.

Where it is used

  • Designing networks of cables, pipes or roads.
  • Clustering: remove the k-1 most expensive links of the tree and you get k groups.
  • Ethernet switches avoid loops with a spanning tree (the Spanning Tree Protocol), though one chosen by switch priorities and path costs rather than a minimum one.
