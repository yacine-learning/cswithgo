History

Richard Bellman developed the method at the RAND Corporation in the early 1950s. In his autobiography he says he chose the name on purpose: the Secretary of Defense, Charles Wilson, disliked the word "research", RAND worked for the Air Force, and Bellman wanted a name for his mathematics that "not even a Congressman could object to". The best-known example in text is the edit distance, defined by Vladimir Levenshtein in Moscow in 1965 while studying codes that correct inserted and deleted symbols.

The problem it fixes

Plain recursion on many problems recomputes the same subproblems an exponential number of times: comparing two words of 10 letters recursively makes millions of calls (exercise 1 counts them).

How it works

Find a way to express the answer from answers to smaller problems of the same kind, then solve each smaller problem once, storing it: in a memo (A2), or in a table filled in an order where every cell needed is already there. For two strings of lengths m and n, the edit distance table has (m+1)(n+1) cells, each computed from three neighbours: O(m × n).

Where it is used

  • Spell checkers and "did you mean…?" (exercise 2).
  • diff and version control compare files line by line with a closely related table (the longest common subsequence).
  • Biology: aligning DNA and protein sequences.
  • Speech recognition and decoding (the Viterbi algorithm), shortest paths with negative costs (Bellman-Ford).
