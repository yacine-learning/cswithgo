History

Early languages such as FORTRAN gave each subroutine one fixed area for its variables, so a subroutine could not call itself. In 1960 John McCarthy's paper on Lisp defined functions recursively, and Edsger Dijkstra published "Recursive Programming", showing how a stack of frames gives every call its own variables; recursion went into ALGOL 60 the same year. In 1968 Donald Michie, who had worked with Turing at Bletchley Park, proposed "memo functions": functions that remember their past results. That is where the word memoization comes from.

The problem it fixes

Many structures are defined in terms of themselves: a folder contains folders, an expression contains expressions, a JSON value contains values. Writing loops over them by hand means managing your own stack.

How it works

A function solves a small case directly (the base case) and a big case by calling itself on smaller pieces. Each call gets its own frame on the call stack (D3), and returns into the caller. When the same smaller calls come back again and again, as in Fibonacci, keep a memo of the answers: each one is then computed once. That is the first step towards dynamic programming (A15).

Where it is used

  • Walking any tree: folders, the parse tree of a compiler, a JSON document (exercise 2).
  • Divide and conquer: merge sort (A6), quicksort (A7), binary search (A3).
  • Depth-first search (A11) and backtracking (A16).
  • Memoization: compilers, parsers (packrat parsing), and caches of pure functions.
