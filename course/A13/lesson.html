History

In 1956 Edsger Dijkstra needed a demonstration for the inauguration of the ARMAC computer in Amsterdam, a problem a non-technical audience would understand: the shortest route between two Dutch cities. One morning, shopping with his fiancée, he sat on a café terrace and designed the algorithm in about twenty minutes, without pencil or paper. He published it three years later, in 1959.

The problem it fixes

BFS (A10) finds the fewest steps, but real links have different costs: a slow link, a long road. The path with the fewest hops is often not the cheapest.

How it works

Always settle the closest node not yet settled: its distance can no longer improve, because any other route would pass through a node that is already farther away. Then update the distances of its neighbours through it. A priority queue (D10) gives the closest node in O(log n): O((nodes + edges) log nodes) in all. It requires costs that are never negative.

Where it is used

  • Link-state routing: OSPF and IS-IS run Dijkstra on every router to compute its routes (exercise 1).
  • Maps and navigation (with improvements such as A*, which adds a guess of the remaining distance).
  • Any "cheapest way from A to B": games, network design, puzzles with move costs.
