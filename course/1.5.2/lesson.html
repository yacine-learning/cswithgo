  • Two indices moving through the data replace a loop inside a loop: O(n) instead of O(n²).
  • From both ends, on sorted data: each step rules out one element for good. You spotted it yourself in February: it is binary search's idea, one element at a time instead of half the range.
  • Same direction, slow and fast: compact in place, remove duplicates, merge two sorted sequences.

The problem

"Are there two requests whose sizes add up to the limit?", "Merge these two sorted logs", "Drop the repeated values". The obvious answer compares every pair: n² comparisons, fine for 100 items, not for a million. The data is sorted, and that is information to use.

Where it comes from

Two pointers is a technique, not one invention, and it has no single author. Its oldest and most famous use is the merge at the heart of merge sort, which John von Neumann wrote in 1945 for the EDVAC (1.6.1): two sorted runs, one index in each, always take the smaller. Every database join of sorted inputs ("merge join") and every sort -m does the same.

The idea

With pointers at both ends of a sorted slice: if a[left] + a[right] is too small, no pair using a[left] can work, since a[right] is the largest partner it has. So drop it: left++. Each step throws away one candidate, so at most n steps.
