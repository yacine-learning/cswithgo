Keep this
  • A hash function turns a key into a bucket number; a lookup goes straight to that bucket instead of scanning: O(1) on average.
  • Different keys can land in the same bucket (a collision): the map keeps them all and compares keys. When buckets get too full (the load factor), the map doubles its buckets and moves every entry.
  • A map has no order. Go randomises iteration on purpose; sort the keys when order matters.

The problem

You have a dictionary of 100 000 words and want the definition of quokka. A list means comparing words until you find it: O(n). A sorted list allows binary search: O(log n), but every insertion has to keep it sorted. You want to go to the right place directly, from the name alone.

Where it comes from

In January 1953 Hans Peter Luhn, an IBM engineer, wrote an internal memo proposing to put records into "buckets" chosen by computing on the key, with the records of a bucket chained together: hashing with chaining, the design of this lesson. Around the same time another IBM group (Amdahl, Boehme, Rochester, Samuel) had the same idea for the IBM 701 assembler. Arnold Dumey published it in 1956, suggesting the remainder of a division by a prime as the hash. Luhn is also the author of the checksum still used on credit card numbers, and in 1958 of the first programs that indexed and summarised documents by counting their words (exercise 3).

Go made map iteration order unpredictable in Go 1 (2012), so that a program silently relying on an order breaks at once, instead of years later on another machine.

The idea

bucket = hash(key) % numberOfBuckets. A good hash spreads keys evenly, so each bucket holds a few entries whatever the size of the map. Keep the average per bucket bounded by growing: doubling the buckets costs a full move of every entry, but it is rare, like doubling a slice (1.2.1), so it is O(1) amortized.

When a map is not O(1)

If every key lands in one bucket, the map is a list: O(n) per lookup. A bad hash does that, and so can an attacker who chooses keys to collide (hash flooding). That is why Go's maps use a random seed per map: the same key does not go to the same bucket in two runs.

Go's real map (since Go 1.24, a "Swiss table") stores entries in groups and probes them with fast bit tricks instead of chaining, but the contract is the same: hash, bucket, compare keys, grow.
