History

The chess composer Max Bezzel published the eight queens puzzle in 1848; Franz Nauck published solutions in 1850, and Carl Friedrich Gauss worked on it too. The word "backtrack" is credited to the American mathematician D. H. Lehmer in the 1950s. In 1972 Edsger Dijkstra used the eight queens to show what he called structured programming, with a detailed depth-first backtracking program.

The problem it fixes

Some problems are a sequence of choices, and only complete sequences can be checked: place eight queens, fill a sudoku, choose values for variables. Trying every combination blindly is hopeless: 16 777 216 boards of eight queens with one per row, but only 92 solutions.

How it works

Make one choice, check that it does not already break a rule, then make the next; when no choice works, undo the last one and try its next option. A dead end costs one step back, not a restart, and a whole branch of impossible boards is cut the moment its first bad choice is made. It is depth-first search (A11) over the tree of choices.

Where it is used

  • Puzzle solvers: sudoku, crosswords, the eight queens (exercise 1).
  • SAT solvers, which decide whether a logical formula can be true, are backtracking with clever pruning.
  • Regular expression engines in Perl and Python backtrack; Go's regexp deliberately does not, which guarantees linear time.
  • Parsers that try one rule and fall back to another.
