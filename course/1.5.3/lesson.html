Keep this
  • Keep a window over a sequence; when it slides, add what enters, remove what leaves. Each element enters once and leaves once: O(n), not O(n × window).
  • Fixed size: moving averages, "requests in the last 60 s", rate limits. Variable size: grow on the right, shrink on the left while the window breaks a rule.
  • TCP's sliding window is the same picture: the bytes sent but not yet acknowledged.

The problem

Your dashboard shows the average latency of the last 100 requests, updated at every request. Recomputing the sum of 100 values each time is 100 additions per request; with a 10 000-sample window and a busy service, the monitoring costs more than the service. Most of the window is the same as a moment ago: only one value entered and one left.

Where it comes from

Like two pointers (1.5.2), it is a technique without a single inventor: it is two pointers moving in the same direction. Its best-known use is in networking: in 1974 Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published the design of TCP, with flow control by a window: the sender may have a window's worth of bytes in flight, and the window slides forward as acknowledgements come back. You have watched it in Wireshark as "win=".

The idea

Keep a running summary of what is inside the window: a sum, a count, a map of what is present. Update it with the one element that enters and the one that leaves. For a time window ("the last 60 seconds"), the elements leaving are the ones that became too old: a queue of timestamps (1.2.4) gives them in order.
