History

Heapsort: J. W. J. Williams invented the binary heap (D10) in 1963 precisely to sort with it, published in 1964. Radix sort is older than computers: Herman Hollerith's punched-card machines tabulated the 1890 US census, and card sorters later sorted a deck one column at a time, from the least significant column to the most. Knuth calls Hollerith's sorting machine the basis of the radix sorting methods used in computers.

The problem it fixes

Quicksort can degrade to O(n²); merge sort needs extra memory. And every comparison sort needs at least about n log₂ n comparisons. Can a sort avoid both problems, or avoid comparing at all?

How it works

Heapsort: build a heap from the slice, then pop the top n times. O(n log n) in every case and in place, but its jumps around memory make it slower than quicksort in practice. Counting sort: when keys are small integers, count how many of each key there are, then write them out in order: O(n + k) for keys below k, no comparisons. Radix sort: sort by the last digit, then the one before, and so on, with a stable counting sort at each step: O(n × digits).

Where it is used

  • Heapsort is the safety net inside Go's pdqsort: when quicksort's pivots keep going badly, it switches to heapsort.
  • Counting sort: histograms, sorting bytes, ranking scores in a small range.
  • Radix sort: fixed-size keys such as integers, IP addresses, dates; suffix arrays in text indexing.

No exercise: once you have a heap (D10) heapsort is ten lines, and counting sort is a loop over a slice of counters. What is worth keeping is the trade-off: heapsort guarantees O(n log n) with no extra memory, and counting and radix sort beat n log n by not comparing at all, when the keys allow it.
