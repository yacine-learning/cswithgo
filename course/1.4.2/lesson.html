  • BFS (breadth-first) explores level by level with a queue (1.2.4): it finds the shortest path counted in edges (hops).
  • DFS (depth-first) goes deep first with a stack or recursion (1.2.3, 1.3.1): reachability, cycles, ordering things that depend on each other.
  • Both need a visited set, or a cycle makes them loop forever. Both are O(nodes + edges).

The problem

Can you turn COLD into WARM by changing one letter at a time, each step a real word, and in how few steps? (Lewis Carroll's word ladders.) Which animals does a poison in the algae of a pond eventually reach? Does a dictionary define a word with words that are themselves defined with that word? All three are walks through a graph, and a graph, unlike a tree, can lead you back where you have already been.

Where it comes from

Depth-first search is older than computers: in the 19th century the French engineer Charles Pierre Trémaux described a way out of any maze by marking the passages you have walked, published by Édouard Lucas in 1882. Breadth-first search was first written by Konrad Zuse in 1945 in his thesis on the Plankalkül language, which was rejected and stayed unpublished until 1972. It was reinvented in 1959 by Edward F. Moore to find the shortest path out of a maze, and in 1961 C. Y. Lee used it to route the wires on circuit boards.

The idea

Same loop, different container. Take a node from the container, visit it, put its unvisited neighbours in. With a queue, the nodes come out in order of distance: everything 1 hop away, then 2 hops. With a stack, the most recent node comes out first, so you dive down one branch before trying the next.

Where they appear in computing

A garbage collector is a graph search: objects are nodes, pointers are edges, and everything not reachable from the program's variables is garbage (module 2). A puzzle solver explores the graph of positions: BFS finds the shortest solution of a sliding puzzle or a Rubik's cube from a given state. With distances on the edges (a road map), the shortest path needs Dijkstra's algorithm, a BFS with a priority queue (1.7.1).
