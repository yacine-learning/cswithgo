History

Königsberg was built around two islands linked by seven bridges, and its people wondered whether one could walk across each bridge exactly once. In 1736 Leonhard Euler proved it impossible by keeping only what mattered: the four land masses as points, the bridges as lines. Each land mass touched an odd number of bridges, and such a walk can have at most two of those, where it starts and ends. That abstraction, points and lines with everything else removed, is the first theorem of graph theory.

The problem it fixes

Lists and trees cannot describe relations where anything can link to anything, in both directions, with loops: who imports whom, which router is wired to which, which page links to which.

How it works

A graph is nodes and edges between them; edges may have a direction and a cost. The usual representation is an adjacency list: for each node, the list of its neighbours, map[string][]string in Go. It takes O(nodes + edges) memory. An adjacency matrix (n × n booleans) answers "is there an edge?" in O(1) but costs n² memory, so it is only worth it for small, dense graphs.

Where it is used

  • Package imports: the Go toolchain refuses import cycles (A11) and builds packages in dependency order (A12).
  • Networks: routers and links (A10, A13).
  • The web: pages and links; Google's PageRank ranked pages from the link graph.
  • Garbage collection: objects and the pointers between them; what is not reachable from the variables is garbage.
