History

René de la Briandais described the structure in 1959 to search files with keys of variable length. In 1960 Edward Fredkin published "Trie Memory" and gave it its name, from retrieval. In 2008 he wrote that he meant it to be pronounced "tree", as in retrieval; most people say "try" to tell it apart from a tree.

The problem it fixes

"Which words start with co?" A hash table cannot answer without looking at every key; a sorted list can, but inserting into it is slow. And many keys share long prefixes, stored again and again.

How it works

A tree where each edge is one letter: the path from the root to a node spells a prefix, and a flag marks the nodes where a whole word ends. Looking up a word of length k takes k steps, whatever the number of words. All the words with a given prefix are the subtree below that prefix's node.

Where it is used

  • Autocomplete in editors, search boxes and phone keyboards (exercise 2).
  • Routers: the longest-prefix match of an IP address against the routing table uses tries on bits.
  • Spell checkers, and the word lists of word games.
  • HTTP routers that match URL paths segment by segment.
